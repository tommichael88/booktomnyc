BTNYC — The Playbook

Vision, principle, and the lessons that built them.

What BTNYC Is

BTNYC is a production home-service quoting platform. Its central question is not "how do we list every service?" — it's:

How do we turn an arbitrary client description into the smallest amount of reliable scope necessary to produce a transparent, accurate, bookable price?

Most platforms solve this one of two ways: catalogue the universe (thousands of SKUs, each requiring its own pricing, intake chain, and maintenance burden), or give up and route everything to "Call us." BTNYC does neither. It models the business — the small, finite set of concepts, behaviors, and conditions that actually determine what work costs — not the SKU. A "Kwikset Halifax Privacy Lever" is a Door Lever with attributes. An "Andersen Window" is a Window. The catalog stays small; coverage is unlimited.

The Vision

Flawless Friction Fulcrum

Every client gets a fully qualified quote on the site, without a pre-visit. The system asks the fewest questions that can retire the uncertainty that actually changes the price — never so many that the client walks away, and never so few that the price is invented. And when the exact price genuinely cannot be known from a distance, the system still offers a real, priced next step rather than a dead-end handoff.

Zero "call us" white flags. Zero system surrender.

One understanding, everywhere — the sentence a customer watches compose is the sentence the price is built from.

The Nine Principles

These are the pillars of the project. Every rule, every chapter, every fix in this document traces back to one of them.

  1. 1Model concepts, not every item in the universe. A finite catalog of behaviors covers an unlimited surface of specific products.
  2. 2One engine, three gateways. Every entry path resolves to the same underlying scope, the same confidence, the same price.
  3. 3Never make the client repeat themselves. What the client has already told us — through their tap, their text, or their answers — is not asked again.
  4. 4Ask only what we need, when we need it. A question exists only if its answer changes price, time, branch, or preparation.
  5. 5Price from reproducible rules. The same resolved scope and inputs always produce the same result.
  6. 6Confidence and complexity are dynamic. The job's shape evolves as evidence arrives; so does the intake, the routing, and the price.
  7. 7Transparency is part of the product. The Ad-Lib sentence summarizes every request in plain English.
  8. 8No white flag. Where an exact price isn't justified, there's still a range, a conditional quote, or a priced diagnostic. Never a dead end. Never a "call us."
  9. 9Converge, verify, preserve. All three gateways converge; every mechanism is verified against the complete set; nothing curated is lost to a compiler.

How This Document Works

The Playbook states durable architectural direction. It is not a status ledger, not a specification, and not a substitute for evidence. This section states how it governs — how rules are classified, how they are enforced, how conflicts are resolved, and what authority the agent has to reshape the system when the system's own shape is the obstacle.

R-GOVERN-DECIDEFIRSTPartialDecide First, Defer Last

Before a decision is filed as needing the operator, its ledger entry carries the measurement that would settle it — taken, not proposed — and the decision the agent will take if it is not answered, under the heading "Default if unanswered". Only a question that turns on information the agent cannot obtain — customer intent, business appetite, legal standing — may be deferred without a measurement, and the entry says which of those it is.

This Rule applies G-GOVERN-AUTONOMY; it does not narrow it. Where an older standing instruction disagrees (the earlier rule that business calls stay excluded even under explicit trust), G-GOVERN-AUTONOMY governs anything reversible and documented. A choice that cannot be undone and could reasonably go either way still stops for the operator, with the preparatory work done and the decision set out.

The mechanical check is a linter over the decisions ledger (PENDING_DECISIONS.md): every open entry numbered after this Rule's adoption carries a non-empty "Default if unanswered" line. Entries that were open at adoption are grandfathered by number and leave that list as they are closed. The linter proves the default exists; it cannot prove the measurement was the right one, which is why the Rule is Partial.

G-GOVERN-SURFACEGovernance ProcessConflict Surfacing

The Charter governs durable direction and decision principles. It is the shared reference, not subordinate to momentary reasoning. When any decision — the agent's own, or one the operator has directed — appears to contradict the Charter, the agent surfaces the conflict, cites the specific rule or principle code, and neither executes nor refuses silently. If the operator confirms the instruction after the conflict is surfaced, the instruction becomes law; the agent executes, then amends the Charter by preserving the original text in a collapsed <details class="amendment-log"> block and recording the new text, the date, and an "Operator Override — Authorized" tag.

When a decision appears to contradict the Charter, the agent surfaces the conflict with a specific code, does not execute silently, and does not refuse silently. If the operator confirms, the instruction becomes law. R-GOVERN-TRANSITIONEnforcedStructural Change Is Authorized

The Charter authorizes reshaping. It does not only protect what exists.

A structural change to the system — the shape of the SSOT, the placement of derived data, the naming of a namespace, the boundary between a service and its parent, the layout of a chain — is an authorized engineering act when the current shape forces a consumer to work around it. Such a change requires three things and no others:

  1. A rationale — the specific workaround the current shape forces. "Consumers must copy this fact into a second location to resolve it correctly" is a rationale. "It feels wrong" is not.
  2. An endpoint — the new shape, stated plainly.
  3. A ledger entry — recorded so the change is discoverable to future sessions.

The change does not require operator sign-off when the rationale is concrete. It does not require a new rule code. It does not require a test in the same change, because it does not change the system's behavior — it changes the shape the behavior is expressed in. The enforcement check asserts that every ledger entry describing a structural change carries both a rationale and an endpoint.

Why this rule exists. Prior to this rule, every norm in the Charter was a preservation norm. Preserve the SSOT's content. Preserve canonical implementations. Preserve the on-return-value provenance. Preserve the fix. There was no affirmative verb for reshaping. When the SSOT's shape was itself the problem, the Charter had nothing to say — and the agent, needing to make progress, either worked around the shape or invented a convention that turned into a rule the Charter never authorized. This rule names the missing act.

G-GOVERN-RULEPARITYGovernance ProcessAll Rules Have Equal Normative Weight

Every Rule has equal normative weight regardless of domain, age, status, or test coverage. Enforced and Partial describe only how completely the current mechanical check covers the Rule; they do not describe authority, priority, importance, or permission to ignore it. No Rule may be selectively enforced because another Rule is considered more important.

G-GOVERN-AUTONOMYGovernance ProcessDecision Authority and Agent Autonomy

Within the Charter, the agent has both the authority and the directive to decide: business numbers, pricing, time estimates, taxonomy, naming, structural placement, and every other choice the Charter does not reserve. Deferral is not the default. Reasoning in place of deciding is a failure mode.

When a real business number is needed, the agent applies the following hierarchy and decides — not defer:

  1. NYC market average. Where a comparable real-world rate exists, use it. Cite the source inline.
  2. Existing catalog data. Where a directly comparable service, modifier, rate, or fee exists in btnyc.json, anchor to it. This includes percentages of an existing rate, values scaled from a comparable modifier, and values anchored to an existing band.
  3. Midpoint of the high/low range. Where neither of the above applies but evidence supports a range, use its midpoint and record the range inline.
  4. Best-considered estimate. Where none of the above applies, make the best-considered estimate and document the reasoning inline.

Every decision made under this authority is documented where it lives — the data field's own _note, a code comment beside the change, or the owning ledger entry — with the reasoning. The agent is not excused from accountability by being empowered to decide.

Standing test."Do I have concrete reasoning grounded in the charter and available evidence, and no operator instruction forbidding it? If yes — decide. If no — the conflict itself is the thing to surface, not the decision."

Beneath Every Group, a Picker

Reaching a group does not produce an undifferentiated grid of services. It produces a picker whose shape reflects what the customer is most likely to already know for this family of work — the parts of door hardware, the symptoms of an appliance, the surfaces of a repair. For a family of work where the customer arrives with a part in mind, the picker leads with parts; for a family where the customer arrives with a problem, it leads with symptoms. The picker honours the shape the customer is actually holding, not the shape the catalog finds convenient.

At the same level as the picker sits its companion "Other" tile — the second door into the same family of work, for the case where the picker's own vocabulary does not cover the customer's job. See Gateway B — The "Other" Tile for what "Other" is and why it is a current part of the product.

Whichever door the customer takes — a specific part, a specific symptom, or "Other" — their choice hands off to the same canonical resolution every other entry path uses. See Convergence — One Destination. How the picker's shape is classified, and how the "Other" tile pairs with it, is stated once in The Navigation Architecture; that chapter is the rule's home, and this chapter references it rather than restating it.

Gateway B — The "Other" Tile

At every group, alongside the behavioral-class-driven picker, sits one more way in. If the picker's own options do not describe the client's actual job — the component is one the catalog has not authored, the symptom is outside the group's known set, or the job is genuinely a different shape from what this group usually handles — the "Other" tile opens a guided builder scoped to that group.

It is not a blank form. It is a structured, step-by-step flow that already knows which group the client came from and asks only what that group still needs answered. It is the same composer underneath — the same sentence machinery, the same passive chips, the same live preview — seeded with the context the client already provided by drilling into the group.

P-CLIENT-OTHERPrincipleThe "Other" tile is a current, intended entry path — not a transitional fallback, and not a legacy escape hatch awaiting retirement. It is the structured complement to behavioral-class-driven navigation, and it is the mechanism by which the system keeps its promise that the catalog stays finite while coverage does not. Where the picker covers the shapes the catalog already knows how to express for this family of work, "Other" covers everything else the group can still legitimately receive — through the same canonical resolution every other path uses. Its role is permanent in the same way the free-text composer is permanent.

Why "Other" Is Current, Not Transitional

The instinct to treat "Other" as a temporary mechanism comes from a reasonable-sounding assumption: that a mature catalog eventually covers every case, so the escape hatch is only needed until the catalog is complete. That assumption is wrong at the level of the business model.

  • The catalog is finite by design. It models concepts, not every product. A customer naming a specific product the catalog has never seen is not a gap in the catalog; it is the catalog working as intended.
  • Behavioral-class-driven navigation is a structured first answer, not an exhaustive one. It covers the shapes the family of work most commonly takes. Anything outside those shapes is not an edge case to be eliminated; it is the reason the "Other" tile exists at the same level.
  • The structured flow is not a degraded experience. A customer reaching the group's builder receives the same quality of intake, the same live preview, the same ad-lib confirmation, the same priced outcome — just reached by a different door.
  • The set of things a customer might describe is unbounded. No picker, however well-designed, can enumerate it. The correct answer to that structural reality is not a bigger picker; it is a structured escape hatch that stays at the same level as the picker itself.

Remove "Other" and the picker becomes a dead end for exactly the cases the system was built to handle. That is the opposite of the product promise.

Group-Scoped, Not a Blank Form

An "Other" tile that opened a blank form would be a much weaker mechanism — it would ask the customer to describe their job from scratch, discarding everything their own navigation already told us. The tile that exists opens a builder scoped to the group the customer came from. It knows the category, it knows the group, and it asks only what the group still needs answered. This is the same inference discipline that governs intake everywhere else: what the customer has already told us through their tap is not asked again.

It Reaches the Same Canonical Outcome

Whatever the group-scoped builder collects, it resolves through the same canonical pipeline every other entry path uses. There is no second resolution pipeline for "Other" and no separate pricing path. See Convergence — One Destination for the invariant, and Dynamic Services & Bespoke Concepts for how the group-scoped dynamic resolution is composed.

Gateway C — SmartQuote

Directly beneath the category grid sits the SmartQuote bar. It is the second thing the client sees, and it is the surface that does the most work. Passive Applicable Chips

As typed text or slot state warrants, related chips appear beneath the input. The user can tap a chip to apply it, and it is appended to the end of their input in the text box. Tapping again — or tapping a selected chip — removes it. Selection and removal are passive: no modal, no navigation, no lost cursor.

Only applicable chips may appear. Chips are drawn from the real smart-tag set in the SSOT, scoped by the applicability model, and surfaced only when the current typed content or slot state actually warrants them. Arbitrary, exhaustive, or "just in case" chip display is prohibited.

Component-Layer Obligations

The layered architecture rules apply without exception:

  • The composer UI is a Renderer. Its state is resolved state, not local mutable business state.
  • Chip applicability is business logic, not rendering logic. The SSOT defines the facts and predicates; a logic/selector layer evaluates them and supplies the renderer with the currently applicable chips. Indexes, memoization, caching, or precomputed lookup structures are implementation details permitted to make that evaluation fast.
  • The preview sentence comes from the canonical ad-lib machinery, not a second sentence generator.
  • No new resolution pipeline. The composer produces or updates canonical context; the orchestrator remains the authority for resolution and pricing.
Prototype is evidence, not architecture. A working interaction prototype can validate the shape of an experience without proving that the production resolution, SSOT, applicability, confidence, or pricing path is complete. The production contract lives in the architecture and its tests. Prototype gaps are implementation evidence to be tracked in the engineering record, not silently promoted into product doctrine.

The Ad-Lib Confirmation

A dynamic, interactive sentence in flawless natural English — not a bullet list — that names every answer the booking context carries and shows how each fee influences the price.

After the pricing engine processes input, it generates a dynamic, interactive ad-lib confirmation sentence. It is the output-side mirror of the "We Understood" preview the client watched compose itself in Gateway C. Same grammar. Same template machinery. Different moment.

  • Linguistic requirement. Flawless, readable, natural English — not a robotic list. It must read like a fluent human summary.
  • Functional requirement. The sentence explicitly validates the user's input by naming the specific answers the booking context carries — whether client-given or system-inferred — and clearly showing how their associated fees influence the final price.
"You need 5 picture frames mounted in your living room. Because we're working with drywall and you'd like same-week scheduling, your quote is $247 — including a $35 drywall-anchor fee (per visit) and $20 rush fee (per visit)."
Chips are not the sentence's vocabulary. The Ad-Lib sentence names answers — the facts the booking context carries. It does not name chips. A chip may be the mechanism by which an answer was produced (a tag firing, synthesizing an answer); the chip itself is never composed into the sentence. The chip row is the chip's home; the sentence is the answer's home.
R-CLIENT-STUTTEREnforcedThe sentence must not stutter. A service's display name can end in the same action word the sentence is about to append ("Cabinet Door or Drawer Adjustment adjusted"). The composer strips a trailing, nominalized action suffix from the label before appending the verb, so the customer reads a clean sentence rather than a doubled word. When the strip cannot apply, the composer does not silently produce the redundancy; the label is either corrected or the sentence structure is chosen to avoid it.

Trust Through Transparency

This sentence is a trust-building mechanism, not a UI flourish. It must drastically reduce uncertainty and make the user feel understood, in control, and respected — not processed.

Design implication. Because the sentence names specific answers and their fees, the underlying pricing must be genuinely explainable in natural language. A pricing formula that cannot produce such a sentence is a signal that the formula is too opaque or the intake is too shallow.

Convergence — One Destination

Three roads, one destination. The destination is not a page. It is a single canonical BookingContext — the contract between resolution and rendering — regardless of which gateway the client came through.

Gateway A:    Minor Home Repairs > Doors > Prehung Door > Install ↓ tile navigation (behavioral-class-driven picker) ↓ same BookingContext → same resolved service Gateway B:    Group-scoped "Other" → guided builder ↓ step-by-step flow ↓ same BookingContext → same resolved service Gateway C:    "I need a Prehung Door Installed in my Bedroom" ↓ composer slots ↓ same BookingContext → same resolved service

All three must converge on the same resolved scope, applicable routing and pricing logic, and customer-facing outcome. They may legitimately collect information differently — one asks a picker question, another a builder question, a third parses it from free text — but for the same facts they must resolve to the same canonical result. Live preview, estimate, and final booking must also converge on that same result. If two paths produce different outcomes for the same facts, the difference is a system defect, not a product variation.

R-CLIENT-CONVERGEEnforcedOne understanding, everywhere. Convergence is not only about the final price. The live preview the customer watches compose must be the same understanding the price is built from. When two parsers exist, they eventually disagree, and the customer is the one who finds out — they watch one understanding compose and receive a different one priced. The single-parse rule in The NLP Layer is the structural answer; this section names the product consequence.

The rule exists because the codebase did not start this way. Three entry paths were added in three different sessions, each solving a different problem, and each quietly re-implementing resolution logic the others already had. Two of the three eventually produced different prices for the same real job. Worse, the free-text path once carried its own, second parser — so the sentence a customer watched compose could be structurally different from the sentence that got priced.

The field-level contract for BookingContext is maintained in COMPONENT_LAYER_MAP.md. The charter commits to the concept — one canonical convergence point between resolution and rendering — not to the specific field names or shapes, which will evolve.

The Final Expression

The system's only job: ask just enough questions so that the resolved scope is sufficient for the applicable pricing path, and if exact scope cannot yet be established, offer a priced path forward — never a dead end.

The finished platform will allow any user to:

  1. Describe their job — via free-text, guided chips, or catalog browsing.
  2. See a transparent, fully editable breakdown — with the ad-lib confirmation as the centerpiece.
  3. Book with confidence — knowing exactly what they're paying for and why.

It scales because it does not catalogue the universe — it models concepts, attributes, and behaviours. It will never say "Call us" without a price.

The Domain Model

BTNYC does not begin with a catalog of services. It begins with the concepts the business can reason about. The domain model is the hierarchy that turns an arbitrary customer phrase into reusable behavior without requiring a bespoke service record for every possible item or wording.

Entity ↓ Behavioral Class ↓ Capability ↓ Strategy ↓ Action ↓ Derived Service

Hierarchy semantics: the arrows mean informs and resolves into, not strict containment. An entity is classified by a behavioral class; the available capabilities constrain which actions are valid; strategy determines how the system gathers evidence; the resolved action and scope produce a derived service expression.

Capability vs. Action: a capability is a reusable statement of what can meaningfully happen to an entity. An action is the concrete operation instantiated for the current job. For example, "replaceable" can be a capability; "replace this door lever" is an action. The words may overlap in casual language, but the model keeps the capability reusable and the action job-specific.

Rule status is not rule priority. Enforced means the current mechanical check covers the Rule's stated claim; Partial means the check covers only the explicitly stated subset. Both are equally binding.

R-DOMAIN-SHAPEEnforcedA Workaround Is Evidence the Shape Is Wrong

A consumer that must work around the SSOT's shape — by copying facts into a second location, by maintaining a parallel representation, by re-deriving what the source could have provided directly — is evidence that the shape is wrong. The response is to change the shape, not to add the workaround.

Workarounds are permitted only as transitions. A transitional workaround is filed as one: its rationale states the shape change that would eliminate it, and the shape change is scheduled. A workaround without a scheduled shape change is a permanent second implementation, and is a defect under R-INVARIANT-CANONICAL.

The enforcement check scans the codebase for the specific shapes workarounds take — a caller-side || or ?? that composes a resolver return with a second source; a second copy of a value that already lives authoritatively elsewhere; an inline re-derivation of a fact that a prior step already resolved — and fails when one appears without a companion ledger entry naming the shape change that will retire it.

The Entity Is the Noun

A customer can name a specific product while the business reasons about the kind of thing it is. A "Kwikset Halifax Satin Nickel Privacy Lever" is still a Door Lever; "Andersen Window" is a Window. The product-specific details remain attributes that can matter to scope or pricing, but they do not require a new conceptual service.

Behavioral Classes Provide Behavior

Behavioral Classes allow unfamiliar phrases to inherit useful behavior from a broader class. A phrase such as "Smart Mirror" does not need a bespoke service entry merely because the exact phrase was not previously catalogued; it can inherit behavior from the appropriate broader behavioral class and enter the same resolution machinery.

Services Are Expressions, Not the Foundation

A service exists so the client can understand and book a resolved piece of work. It should not become the place where the business's entire knowledge is duplicated. The same underlying entity, capability, strategy, and action can produce different customer-facing expressions as scope requires.

P-DOMAIN-SCOPEPrincipleFact Scoping Is Explicit at Three Levels

A recognized fact — a smart tag, in the SSOT's own vocabulary — is not a universal statement. It is a fact about a shape of work, and its applicability is declared at three narrowing levels:

The three levels are deliberate, and they exist because scoping is a genuine tradeoff. A fact scoped too broadly touches work it has nothing to say about; a fact scoped too narrowly has to be re-declared for every sibling service. The exclusion layer exists because a real group can genuinely contain both the fact-applying and the fact-not-applying case — and the honest answer is neither to remove the fact from the whole group nor to author a second, less specific version of it. It is to say, at the group level, "this applies, except here."

Dynamic Services & Bespoke Concepts

The catalog is finite. The set of things a client might ask for is not. BTNYC resolves this tension by modeling the business — the small, finite set of concepts, behaviors, and conditions that determine what work costs — rather than cataloguing every specific product. When a client names something the catalog has never seen, the system does not fail; it finds the closest behavioral class and prices the work from there.

A dynamic service is a real, priceable resolution that was not authored as its own named service entry. It has the same shape as a named service — an entity, a routing mode, a capability, a strategy, an action, a price — but it is composed at resolution time from the parts the catalog already has. A "smart mirror" is a mirror. A motorized-curtain request is a curtain installation with an electromechanical nuance the intake can capture.

Two of the three gateways reach dynamic services directly. The "Other" tile is the explicit entry path — a customer who drills into a group and does not find their job in the picker opens a builder scoped to that group, and the job resolves as a dynamic service within it. Free text reaches the same mechanism when the client's phrase maps to a behavior without mapping to a named service. The Ad-Lib Confirmation then names what was resolved in the same way it would name any other service: as a scope, not as a catalog record.

The Finite Catalog Thesis, Expanded

The platform does not attempt to enumerate the universe. It does not seek to catalog:

  • Every product
  • Every brand
  • Every model
  • Every entity
  • Every phrase
  • Every customer description

That set is unbounded. Instead the platform catalogs:

  • Behaviors
  • Capabilities
  • Conditions
  • Environments
  • Cost drivers
  • Uncertainty classes

This distinction is what allows a finite system to provide effectively unlimited coverage.

P-DOMAIN-UNCERTAINTYPrincipleCoverage scales through uncertainty modeling rather than exhaustive enumeration. The platform does not attempt to catalog every possible entity. It attempts to catalog the finite set of conditions that materially influence how work is priced, routed, scheduled, scoped, or prepared. There are infinitely many entities. There are not infinitely many reasons work becomes more expensive, takes longer, requires different preparation, or follows a different pricing path. Coverage scales through uncertainty modeling rather than entity enumeration.

Bespoke Work Is Not a Failure Condition

A bespoke request is evidence that the domain model is functioning correctly. The platform does not need perfect recognition of every object. The platform must identify the uncertainty classes that determine how the work should be scoped and priced. Novel entities therefore enter the same uncertainty-resolution machinery as familiar entities.

A previously unseen object may still be:

  • Mounted
  • Repaired
  • Installed
  • Removed
  • Diagnosed

The object's uniqueness does not require a unique pricing architecture. Only genuinely new uncertainty classes require expansion of the model.

The Shape of a Dynamic Service

Every dynamic service is keyed by its scope, not by its name. Two key shapes exist, and only these two:

A dynamic service declares a full pricing shape, a checkout state, an intake chain, and a confidence strategy — the same fields a named service carries. What it does not declare is a client-facing name of its own. The name is derived from scope. The same scope renders the same name regardless of entry path.

The merge_strategy field governs how a dynamic service's own declarations relate to the group it belongs to. A value of "extend" means the dynamic service's own chain adds to whatever the group's default already contains; a future "replace" would override it. Both semantics are data, not code — the orchestrator reads the field and applies the strategy, rather than hardcoding a merge rule per group.

What Makes a Dynamic Service Honest

The Fallback Chain

When a request reaches the dynamic resolution machinery, it descends a short, auditable ladder. Each rung is a real, explicit rule — not a silent attempt:

Free-text or Other-tile request ↓ Resolve a named service?  ──── yes ────→ the named service ↓ no Resolve a group + reasonable confidence?  ──── yes ────→ synthesise the group's Other tile ↓ no Resolve a category at all?  ──── yes ────→ generic wizard for that category ↓ no Default fallback category, service_type, base_price

Each tier keeps whatever the customer already told us. If the group is known but no specific service is, the system opens the group's guided builder — not a blank form. If only the category is known, the generic wizard asks which group. If nothing is known, the fallback still carries a category, a service type, and a base price — the customer never reaches an unpriced dead end.

P-DOMAIN-FALLBACKPrincipleThe fallback never invents certainty it does not have. Each rung of the ladder corresponds to a real level of confidence about what the job is. The system steps down only as far as it must, and every question it asks at each level is a question that level still needs answered.

When a Phrase Deserves a Named Service

The boundary between named services and dynamic resolution is a design decision, not a technical one. Some behaviors deserve to be named — because they carry their own specific intake requirements, their own pricing shape, or their own customer-facing vocabulary. Others do not; they are covered generically, and the system's coverage scales with the breadth of its behavioral model rather than the depth of its catalog.

A phrase deserves its own named service when at least one of the following is true:

  • It has a distinct, authored pricing shape that a generic formula cannot express (the PAX wardrobe formula; the tile repair formula).
  • It carries a distinct, real intake requirement that no sibling service can share (a fixture-specific question; a material-specific one).
  • It has its own, named, client-facing vocabulary — a customer says "the dishwasher repair," not "a sink repair that involves a dishwasher."
  • It carries a regulatory, safety, or warranty distinction that must be honoured separately.
The judgment is not "can this be a dynamic service?" — almost anything can. The judgment is "does the client experience improve, or does the pricing become more honest, by authoring a real service here?" When the answer is no, a dynamic service with a well-authored chain is the better answer, because it stays in sync with the concepts the rest of the catalog already expresses.

What Dynamic Services Buy

The mechanism keeps the catalog honest at three levels simultaneously:

  • Coverage without sprawl. A customer naming a "smart mirror" or a "dining nook" does not require a product-team decision to author a new service. The behavioural model covers it, and the dynamic service expresses the resolved work.
  • Consistency under ambiguity. Two customers describing the same job in different words reach the same dynamic service, because both were routed by the same group-and-routing-mode logic. The "Other" tile is not a blank form; it is a constrained composer.
  • Honest scope across entry paths. The dynamic service's own pricing shape is the same one a named service would use. A dynamic service is not a degraded second class — it is the same class, composed differently.

The Two Failure Modes Worth Naming

Two failure modes recur, and both have a real, checkable answer.
  • The naked quantity trap. A dynamic service whose only question is "how many?" under a symptom-first or component-first group. The fix is to ask the shape question first, then quantity if the shape still warrants it. A dynamic service with a bare count is a defect, not a simplification.
  • The cross-category bleed. A dynamic service authored under one group whose chain carries questions from another category's vocabulary. The fix is to check the chain against the group's own real services, not against the category's general fallback.

A dynamic service is real when it is reachable, its chain matches its routing mode, and its pricing shape is honest. Those three tests are the whole mechanism.

A dynamic service is only as honest as the questions it asks. If a group declares itself symptom_first, but the chain it runs asks only a quantity, the customer's actual problem never gets scoped. Enforcing the pairing is what makes "the catalog stays finite, coverage does not" a design fact rather than a wish.

The Multi-Dimensional Complexity Model

Complexity is not a score.

Complexity is not confidence.

Complexity is not uncertainty.

Complexity is the set of characteristics that determine how much uncertainty can legitimately exist about a job.

The platform's purpose is not to determine whether a job is complex. The platform's purpose is to determine what must be known in order to price the job honestly.

Complexity therefore exists because it exposes uncertainty. The relationship is:

Complexity ↓ Exposes Uncertainty ↓ Requires Evidence ↓ Creates Confidence ↓ Unlocks Pricing Precision

This sequence is the canonical relationship between Complexity, Confidence, and Friction.

Complexity never directly lowers confidence. Complexity increases the amount of uncertainty that may legitimately exist. The confidence system itself remains unchanged. Only the uncertainty landscape changes.

P-CONF-COMPLEXITYPrincipleComplexity does not directly reduce confidence. Complexity increases the amount of uncertainty that may legitimately exist. More complex work therefore requires retirement of more uncertainty before the same pricing precision is justified. The confidence model remains constant. Only the uncertainty landscape changes.

Complexity Is Not One Number

A job's complexity is a position in a multi-dimensional space. Potential dimensions include:

  • Volume — how many units.
  • Access — floor level, staircase, elevator availability, doorway width, tight-corner reach. Parking is a real, price-affecting access factor, but it is never asked — it is captured structurally.
  • Environment — flooring to protect, finishes to avoid, prep required.
  • Material handling — on-site vs. transport; known vs. to-be-sourced; stock vs. custom/special-order size.
  • Scope of work — swap / install / repair / install-with-new-construction.
  • Trip count — single-visit vs. multi-visit.
  • Structural conditions — framing, substrate, load, existing installation quality.
  • Installation conditions — the specific physical facts of this installation vs. a generic one.
  • Safety considerations — height, electrical, gas, load, regulatory.

No single dimension determines complexity. Two jobs may belong to the same nominal service while occupying entirely different positions within this space. The platform therefore reasons about uncertainty rather than attempting to collapse every job into a universal complexity score.

Two Real ExamplesWhere the scalar model breaks
"Routine" is not always routine. A standard cabinet knob install is genuinely routine. A 400-knob installation for a new housing development — new holes on every one, template drilling, multiple trips, bulk materials ordering, and possibly holes already butchered by a contractor the builder fired mid-project — is also nominally a knob install. Same service, same nominal tier, nothing about it is routine.

"Skilled" is not always equivalent. A standard interior door install is Skilled. A 14" closet door into a bedroom cubby corner — new trim work, door transport from the shop, drywall mudding, multiple trips, a second-floor staircase with a heavy door, white carpet in the work area — is nominally the same tier and the same service. It is substantially more time-consuming, more materially involved, and more coordination-heavy.

Complexity Generates Uncertainty

A routine request exposes relatively few uncertainty classes. A complex request exposes more.

A simple wall-mounted picture frame may expose:

  • Wall type
  • Quantity

A custom exterior doorway installation may expose:

  • Framing condition
  • Structural opening condition
  • Material requirements
  • Weatherproofing requirements
  • Access restrictions
  • Specialty preparation requirements

The confidence model remains identical. Only the number and type of uncertainty classes changes.

Confidence & Uncertainty

Confidence & Uncertainty

Price cannot be known until complexity is known. Complexity cannot be known until enough information is available. But asking for information has a cost.

The system asks only enough questions to close the confidence gap required for the price — and no more. This is the mathematical heart of the entire platform.

Confidence Is Not Probability

BTNYC does not model confidence as a probability.

The platform does not attempt to predict:

"There is an 82% chance this quote is correct."

Instead it models:

"82% of the pricing-relevant uncertainty required for this pricing path has been retired."

This distinction is deliberate.

Probability attempts to predict correctness. Confidence measures evidence completeness.

The system does not claim it knows whether unseen conditions exist. It claims it knows which meaningful uncertainties remain unresolved.

P-CONF-UNCERTAINTYPrincipleConfidence is not a prediction. Confidence is the proportion of relevant uncertainty retired. The system never expresses confidence as a statistical claim regarding quote correctness. Confidence is an evidence-completeness measure.

Uncertainty Is the Primitive

Customers do not pay for questions. Customers pay for work.

Questions exist only because they retire uncertainty about work. The platform therefore tracks uncertainty directly. Confidence is derived from uncertainty retirement. Questions are merely one mechanism that may retire uncertainty. Photographs, documents, model numbers, previous answers, navigation context, and inferred facts may do the same.

Intent Confidence and Scope Confidence

The platform maintains two separate confidence measures.

  • Intent confidence (Cᵢ) answers: Do we understand what work the customer wants?
  • Scope confidence (Cₓ) answers: Do we understand the conditions under which that work will occur?

These concerns are intentionally separate.

A request may have high Intent Confidence and low Scope Confidence. The platform may know exactly what the customer wants while knowing very little about the conditions that determine cost.

Notation.Cᵢ is intent confidence on a 0–1 scale; Cₓ is scope confidence on a 0–1 scale; τₓ is the required scope-confidence threshold for the active resolution/pricing policy. These are normalized confidence measures, not probabilities of an outcome.

Confidence Is Not Question Count

Confidence must never be calculated from questions answered divided by total questions. This calculation is prohibited.

Questions are not inherently valuable. Only uncertainty retirement is valuable. Five low-impact answers may retire less uncertainty than one high-impact answer. The system therefore evaluates uncertainty classes rather than questionnaire completion.

P-CONF-NOQUESTIONMATHPrincipleConfidence is not derived from question counts. Confidence derives from retired uncertainty. A service whose client answered more questions is not necessarily better understood. Question quantity and confidence are not equivalent.

Uncertainty Classes

The platform reasons about uncertainty through uncertainty classes. An uncertainty class represents a condition whose answer could materially affect:

  • Price
  • Labor
  • Routing
  • Scheduling
  • Preparation

Examples:

Dynamic Uncertainty

Uncertainty is not fixed. The customer's answers may reveal additional uncertainty. The platform therefore permits confidence to decrease.

Example:

  1. Initial request: Mount a television. Potential uncertainties: Wall type; Television size; Mount availability.
  2. Customer later answers: Above a fireplace. Additional uncertainties emerge: Masonry drilling; Heat exposure; Elevated mounting conditions; Cable-routing constraints.

Total uncertainty increases. Confidence may decline.

This behavior is correct. The system understands the job more accurately than it did previously.

P-CONF-DYNAMICPrincipleConfidence may decrease as evidence arrives. Evidence may reveal new uncertainty classes. When total relevant uncertainty increases, confidence may legitimately decline. The objective is not monotonic confidence growth. The objective is accurate representation of remaining uncertainty.

The Job Shape Is Dynamic

The selected service does not determine the complete intake by itself. As the client answers questions, the system may learn that the actual job is simpler, more complex, or materially different from the initial baseline. The required questions therefore follow the evolving shape of the actual job, not merely the name of the service that started the flow.

The Seesaw

Cᵢ high → service identified ↓ Cₓ low  → high uncertainty (ask for evidence) ↓ Cₓ rises → complexity becomes calculable ↓ Cₓ ≥ threshold → price unlocked, uncertainty retired
Friction = max(0, τₓ − Cₓ) — a scope-friction metric measuring the remaining gap between required scope confidence and current scope confidence. It intentionally measures scope work only; intent confidence is handled separately by the routing/safety gate. If intent is insufficient, the system does not treat high Cₓ as permission to invent a service.

Scope Confidence

Conceptually:

Cₓ = Retired Relevant Uncertainty / Total Relevant Uncertainty

The denominator is dynamic. It expands and contracts as the actual shape of the work becomes known. The platform measures progress toward understanding the actual job, not progress toward completing a fixed questionnaire.

R-CONF-ONEFORMULAPartialOne Formula, Everywhere

There is one confidence formula, not three. The orchestrator's canonical confidence function is the single implementation. The legacy curated-card path used to carry its own, third, hand-rolled formula; that formula is retired. Where the two paths still compute confidence, they compute the same number from the same inputs, and any divergence is a tracked defect rather than a design variation.

The single formula is entry-type-aware. A direct catalog tap — where the customer chose the service by name — reaches the top of the score immediately, because intent is certain by construction. A free-text match earns its score from the NLP match strength and from the answers the customer provides. An "Other" tile resolution earns its score from the category and group context the customer's own navigation supplied. The formula's behaviour at the top of the range is deliberately conservative: a request whose evidence comes entirely from a weak signal cannot reach a named-service commitment on that evidence alone.

The Thresholds

Confidence thresholds are policy inputs used by the resolution engine; they determine when a particular pricing path has enough scope to proceed at the level of precision it promises. Their exact values, tier boundaries, and tuning belong in the owning specification and decision record, not in this Playbook.

How τₓ is set today: τₓ is not derived from a universal scalar complexity score. The active threshold is an explicit policy input associated with the applicable resolution/pricing tier in the SSOT and owning specification. The multi-dimensional complexity model describes the job shape that informs that policy; it does not currently collapse every job into one universal scalar. If the project adopts a different mapping, that mapping is an explicit specification decision and this charter must be updated with it.

P-CONF-PRECISIONPrincipleConfidence gates precision, not conversion. A confidence gate may prevent an exact quote from being presented prematurely. It must not be used as a generic excuse to stop the customer from reaching a priced outcome.

Pricing Precision

Confidence gates precision. Confidence does not gate conversion.

Different pricing outputs require different levels of uncertainty retirement:

  • Exact Quote
  • Bounded Range
  • Conditional Quote
  • Diagnostic Path

The system's responsibility is selecting the most precise outcome justified by available evidence. The customer always receives a priced path forward.

P-CONF-SAFETYPrincipleThe Safety Net

Intent confidence alone is not sufficient to auto-select a specific named service. The orchestrator needs sufficient deterministic evidence to commit to that scope. When it does not have that evidence, it should stay at the appropriate dynamic, category, or diagnostic level rather than overstate certainty.

Free text may contribute evidence, but no single weak signal should be allowed to masquerade as complete scope. The exact evidence rules and confidence coefficients belong to the resolution specification.

Friction & Uncertainty Retirement

The confidence model answers how much uncertainty must be retired. The economic model answers whether retiring that uncertainty is worth the friction it costs the client. These are related but distinct questions.

Friction Exists to Retire Uncertainty

Friction is not caused by questions. Friction is caused by customer effort. Questions are one form of customer effort. Photographs are another. File uploads are another. Measurements are another.

The platform therefore evaluates friction by asking:

How much uncertainty does this action retire?

not

How much information does this action collect?

Information is not the objective. Retiring meaningful uncertainty is.

The Friction-Efficiency Principle

The optimal interaction is not the one that collects the most information. The optimal interaction retires the most meaningful uncertainty with the least customer effort. The platform therefore prefers high-density evidence whenever business rules permit it.

R-FRICTION-RETIREPartialFriction is justified only when the uncertainty retired exceeds the customer effort required. A question may be useful without being justified. The system seeks maximum uncertainty retirement per unit of client effort. Information collection is not the objective. Uncertainty retirement is.
P-FRICTION-NOTBLINDPrincipleDo not minimize friction blindly. Minimize unnecessary friction while protecting the business from uncertainty that materially changes price, labor, schedule, or outcome. The right amount of friction is the least client effort that retires the risks that actually matter.

High-Density Evidence

Certain forms of evidence may retire multiple uncertainty classes simultaneously. Examples:

  • Photographs
  • Videos
  • Manufacturer labels
  • Model numbers
  • Existing installation photos

A photograph may retire more uncertainty than multiple text questions. When supported by service rules, the platform should prefer high-density evidence over equivalent low-density questionnaires.

The Economic Balance

The platform does not seek:

Minimum Friction

Nor does it seek:

Maximum Confidence

It seeks:

Minimum Friction necessary to achieve the confidence required for the intended pricing precision.

Anything beyond that is unnecessary friction. Anything below that risks invented precision.

P-FRICTION-SKIPPEDPrincipleDurable Rules

  • A question is justified only when its answer can change price, time, branch, or preparation.
  • A skipped answer must remain unknown; the system must never silently invent certainty.
  • If uncertainty remains material, the system may move to a range, conditional quote, or priced diagnostic rather than blocking the client.
  • High-density evidence such as a photograph may replace several lower-value questions when the service's rules support that inference.
  • Any risk markup, scheduling buffer, confidence coefficient, or numeric threshold is an SSOT coefficient or an explicit business decision, not a charter constant.

Intake
What we ask. What we refuse to ask. How the chain evolves as answers arrive.

Intake Question Design Discipline

The following rules govern any authored intake chain. They are the canonical statement — a future conversation that conflicts with them should be checked against them, not assumed to supersede them.

P-INTAKE-QTESTPrincipleThe Question Test

Before asking any intake question:

Is this absolutely necessary, highly targeted, and not already answerable from information the system already has?

A question must not be asked if:

  1. the client already answered it in free text;
  2. the navigation path already supplied the fact;
  3. another answer already makes it redundant;
  4. the service itself makes the answer effectively obvious.

R-INTAKE-WIREREMOVEPartialThe Wire-or-Remove Test

Every question in any intake chain must pass at least one of the following. A question that passes none must be removed or made display-only.

  1. Price — its answer changes the final price through a genuinely-consulted pricing mechanism.
  2. Time — its answer changes the estimated labor time.
  3. Branch — its answer determines which subsequent question(s) appear, and at least one of those passes this same test.
  4. Prep — its answer captures information a technician genuinely needs before arriving, not already inferable from navigation context.

Specific violation classes:

  • Location class. Asking location on services where the location is structurally constant (a dishwasher is always in a kitchen) or already implied by navigation path. Pure friction.
  • Setup-module class. A computer/TV setup question must not appear in the intake chain of any service outside its own category.
  • Force-injection class. A module force-injected into every service in a tier, regardless of relevance to the specific service, is almost always a violation. The bar for a universal question is much higher than the bar for a scoped one.

Wire-or-Remove scope: a question is wired only when its answer cannot already be inferred from navigation, free text, or structural knowledge and the answer can change the resolved outcome.

P-INTAKE-INFERPrincipleThe Inference Rules

  1. Free-text inferability. If NLP-detected tags or parsed entities already carry the answer (e.g. the client typed "brick wall"), do not re-ask it as a structured question.
  2. Navigation-path inferability. If reaching a service required tapping through a sub-group that already declares the answer (e.g. a "Brick" sub-group before a repair service), the system already knows that fact structurally.
  3. Redundancy. If another question in the same chain already resolves it, do not ask it again, even if worded differently.
  4. Structural availability. If the fact is a structural property of the job — parking access, urgency for an emergency service, whether a dishwasher is in a kitchen — it must not be asked. It is captured from the service itself, the navigation path, or free text, and applies as a modifier when known. The question does not exist.
The specific, most-frustrating failure mode these rules exist to prevent: when the client has already told us the answer through their navigation, and the intake asks them anyway.

A client taps through a specific path — brick wall, hourly-billed, frames. The system now knows, with certainty, four facts about the job. Then the intake asks "what wall are we drilling into?" (brick — already told), "what are you hanging?" (a frame — already told), "do you need a 50-ft ladder?" (this is a picture frame at eye level — obviously not).

This is the intake chain being asinine. The narrow, correct behavior is: the system asks only what it cannot infer.

Question-Count Ranges

Every service should have an authored minimum and an allowed range rather than a single fixed intake length. The system chooses where the actual request falls within that range from the information already known and the way the job evolves.

  • Base scope: the minimum information verified as sufficient for a standard request.
  • Escalation: additional questions become available only when the client's facts diverge from the expected job shape or expose a material pricing, labor, schedule, or preparation risk.
  • Authoring obligation: the authored chain must be capable of reaching the required resolution state for the jobs it claims to cover.
  • Scope-changing branch: when an answer reveals a genuinely different job shape, a new branch may have its own intake requirements; those questions are not simply "extra" questions on the original job.

The Playbook does not prescribe a universal numeric question ceiling. Friction protection is a design invariant; the exact ranges and limits belong in the owning service/routing specification.

The scope-changing-branch exception

One class of question sits outside the tier cap. A question whose answer opens an entirely new sub-scope of work is not a longer version of the same job — it is the questions of a different job the client's own answer has revealed. The canonical case: a prehung interior door install normally asks two questions. If the client answers that they need a custom door size — a size no prehung unit is manufactured in — the branch that opens is a second job's worth of scoping. Its questions render even though the base tier cap is two.

The distinction is strict. A question is a scope-changing branch if and only if its answer changes the shape of the work — a different material, a different action, a different entity. A question that merely clarifies a parameter of the same job is subject to the tier cap normally.

The Confidence Baseline

Every service has an implicit baseline: what a standard request for that service looks like. Then, as the client answers:

  • Answers that confirm the baseline raise confidence — the system already knows the shape of the job, and can ask fewer questions.
  • Answers that diverge from the baseline lower confidence — the job is no longer what the system expected, and the system must ask more questions to scope it correctly.

Two clients requesting the nominally "same" service can receive different, correct question sets — one shorter, one longer — both reaching 100% confidence for their respective actual jobs.

The canonical example — a prehung interior door install:

If the client says "upstairs bathroom" — location context infers solid-core construction. Standard job. Confidence rises. We skip the door-type question entirely.

If the client says "bedroom closet, 14 inches, white carpet" — the baseline has changed. Confidence drops. We now need more questions.

Same nominal service. Same nominal tier. Radically different actual jobs.

Questions Should Be Customer-Answerable

A technically useful question is not automatically a good customer question. Prefer decisions the client can reasonably make over internal measurements or terminology they should not have to calculate. When the system needs a technical distinction, translate it into a familiar choice or use another available source of evidence when the business rules support doing so.

Quantity Questions

Routine services: the only question asked should be total quantity — plus, where a real, named exception applies, one additional question that changes the cost of the work itself.

Skilled / Specialized services: quantity should generally not be asked at all. Each unit of a genuinely complex job carries too much of its own nuance to be meaningfully batched by a count.

The "no, but not never" rule. Quantity may legitimately be asked of a complex job only when the client themselves explicitly states it:

  • Never in a catalog grid tap. Tapping a tile is the client saying "this one"; there is no quantity signal.
  • Possibly if the client explicitly selects a quantity in the Guided Builder.
  • Possibly if the client writes a quantity in the free-text input and the parser extracts it.
R-INTAKE-QTYFIELDPartialQuantity presentation is one field, presented once, editable everywhere it appears. Regardless of which entry path authored it, the value must land on one field on the booking context, written by exactly one authoritative code path per entry route, and be presented to the client in one unmistakable, easily-changeable place. It must never appear twice, never contradict itself across views.
R-INTAKE-QTYONCEPartialQuantity must not double-count. A service that already carries its own, dedicated quantity question captures the count through that question. The outer quantity multiplier must not then apply on top of it. When a service's chain genuinely does not address quantity, the outer multiplier is the correct mechanism — and only then. The decision is made once, at the resolution boundary, and applied consistently by every caller.
Part VII
No White Flag
Never a dead end. Every uncertainty resolves to a priced path forward.

P-WHITEFLAG-PRICEDPrincipleNo White Flag — Priced Paths Forward

Never a dead end. If the resolved scope supports an exact price, present it. If exactness is not justified, the system may present an explicit range, a conditional quote, or a registered diagnostic service. There is always a priced path forward.

Precision levels. For this Playbook, precision means the specificity of the priced outcome presented to the client: exact quote → bounded range → conditional quote → registered diagnostic path. Confidence determines which level is justified; it does not determine whether the client gets a priced next step.

The important distinction is that confidence gates the level of precision, not the existence of a price. The orchestrator does not need to force low-confidence work through the exact-quote formula. It resolves the appropriate pricing path: exact quote, bounded/conditional quote, or diagnostic pricing. Each path is reproducible and bookable on its own terms.

P-WHITEFLAG-DIAGPrincipleDiagnostic pricing is a real pricing method. A fixed diagnostic fee is not a magic number emitted when confidence fails. It is a registered service/pricing shape with explicit scope, price, and booking behavior.

P-WHITEFLAG-PRECISEPrincipleNo Invented Precision

Uncertainty does not authorize invented precision. If a range is shown, its bounds must come from explicit, reproducible scenarios or modifiers supported by the pricing model. The Playbook does not require a probability distribution or a weighted expected value.

When the remaining uncertainty is too material for an exact quote, the system may instead resolve to a registered diagnostic service with its own explicit price. That price is not a fallback number manufactured by the confidence engine; it is the price of a real, bookable diagnostic path.

The Fork — Two Paths to Conversion

Path A — Remote Guided Intake (Deep Dive)

Trigger: use this path when the system has identified the work sufficiently to know which additional remote evidence can materially reduce remaining scope uncertainty.

Expose deeper, more technical questions. Value prop: "Save money by providing more detail." Outcome: If successful, the seesaw balances; exact price appears.

Path B — On-Site Pro (Diagnostic Escape Hatch)

Trigger: use this path when remaining uncertainty is material enough that further remote intake is not justified or cannot establish the confidence required by the applicable exact-quote path, and a registered diagnostic service is available.

When remote scope cannot be established to the level required for an exact quote, the system may offer a priced diagnostic visit as the next legitimate service. The customer receives a concrete, bookable outcome rather than an unpriced handoff. If the business offers a diagnostic-fee credit toward subsequent work, that credit is an explicit pricing/business rule, not an implicit consequence of low confidence.

P-WHITEFLAG-FORKBPrinciplePath B must book the fee it promised. When the fork offers the customer an on-site diagnostic at a stated flat fee, selecting Path B must produce a bookable route carrying that exact fee — not the service's general hourly estimate, not a different checkout state, not an unpriced request. The fee the customer read is the fee the booking carries. If the underlying service's default checkout state would show a different number, the fork's own path overrides it, because the fork is the moment the customer chose this path on these terms.

Eligibility, diagnostic pricing, credit policy, and the exact UI belong to the owning specification. The architectural rule is simply that uncertainty resolves to a real priced path, never to a disguised "Call us" state.

Why the Fork Exists

The fork preserves the core product promise when remote scoping reaches its practical limit: the client still gets a concrete, bookable next step instead of an unpriced handoff.

Architecture, not status: the fork is a product pattern, not a promise that every service currently exposes both paths. Eligibility and implementation coverage belong in the engineering record and owning specification.
Part VIII
Pricing
A finite set of pricing methods. One reproducible pipeline. Coefficients belong in the SSOT.

The Pricing Pipeline

Regardless of the particular pricing shape, the durable pipeline is: resolve the applicable method, apply the scoped inputs, calculate the price, apply explicit per-visit fees and materials according to their declared scope, derive the client-facing checkout state, and expose the same result to the client and booking flow.

Resolved scope ↓ Pricing shape / method ↓ Scoped quantities + modifiers ↓ Labor / base calculation ↓ Per-visit fees + materials + surcharges ↓ Checkout state / explanation ↓ Bookable price

Checkout state is presentation/output. It communicates the justified precision of the pricing result; it does not calculate the price and is not itself a pricing shape.

  • Scope is explicit. Per-unit and per-visit effects must never be conflated.
  • Pricing is reproducible. The same resolved scope and inputs produce the same result.
  • The UI does not invent prices. Rendering consumes the resolved pricing result.
  • Preview and finalization converge. The number shown during scoping must come from the same pricing path used when the job is committed.

Exact formula stages, field names, and coefficients are implementation specification, not charter doctrine. They belong in the SSOT, schema, and owning engineering documentation.

The Pricing Shape Registry

Pricing is not one universal equation with arbitrary switches. BTNYC uses a finite set of reproducible pricing methods, each suited to a different physical reality of work. The active registry and its exact names belong to the SSOT and its schema; this charter defines the rule that every price must resolve through an explicit, registered method.

R-PRICE-REGISTEREDPartialA new pricing method is an engine change, not a new data value. A new coefficient, modifier, fee, quantity band, or service mapping should normally be data. A genuinely new formula shape requires an explicit implementation change, schema/validator coverage, and regression protection.

The registry the SSOT actually carries today is small. The Playbook names the shapes, not their coefficients:

ShapeMethod (conceptual)When it fires
flat_simpleA single, fixed base price with modifiers applied and quantity handled as an outer multiplierMost named services and flat-rate dynamic services
hourly_timedBase + extra fee + (minutes / 60) × tier rate, times quantity, plus per-visit feesServices whose real cost is time × rate
diagnostic_openThe diagnostic fee alone, credited toward repair if the customer proceedsServices where remote scope genuinely cannot be established
formulaDelegates to a named, registered entry in pricing_formulas for its complete calculationServices whose pricing shape is genuinely distinct (PAX wardrobe; hardware install; tile repair)

The detailed live registry belongs with btnyc.json, btnyc_schema.json, and the pricing implementation. Keeping the complete list out of this charter prevents a routine pricing-data change from making the Playbook stale.

Diagnostic pricing status: "diagnostic" is a recognized pricing shape in this architecture, but the live diagnostic service names, prices, eligibility rules, and any credit policy remain registered in the SSOT. The charter does not invent a particular diagnostic service or coefficient.

The Formula Shape

Pricing is a reproducible, data-driven method — not a rigid flat rate, and not a catalogue of numbers.

Price = max(base_labor, (base_minutes + Σ(quantity × per_unit_minutes) + Σ(modifier_minutes)) × hourly_rate) + flat_fees + materials + surcharges

The Three Components

  1. Base labor — irreducible effort: arrival, setup, safety, minimum billable work.
  2. Quantity-per-unit labor — a per-piece increment that scales continuously. Never stepped. Stepping creates artificial price cliffs.
  3. Complexity modifiers — conditional adjustments tied to intake answers reflecting real, physical differences in work.

Per-Visit vs. Per-Unit

Some costs genuinely scale per unit. Others are one-time, per-visit costs regardless of how many units are worked on in the same trip.

R-PRICE-SCOPEEnforcedThe rule: every modifier carries an explicit scope: "per_unit" | "per_visit". Per-visit fees are added once, outside the quantity multiplier. Per-unit fees are multiplied inside it.
The failure mode this rule prevents: a single, one-time surcharge — an urgency fee, a parking fee, an access fee — being charged once per door because the whole fee sum was multiplied by quantity. The rule is not cosmetic; it is the difference between a quote that reflects one visit and a quote that reflects ten. When a modifier's scope is wrong, the error scales linearly with quantity and is invisible at qty=1.

High-Volume Overflow

When piece count exceeds the quantity covered by the service's baseline flat rate, the service's declared overflow modifier applies. The phrase roughly one hour of labor describes the current conceptual baseline only; the actual baseline quantity/minutes and coefficient are configurable SSOT data, not a charter constant.

  • Additional units beyond baseline use the service's declared overflow modifier.
  • The exact coefficient belongs in the SSOT, not in this charter.

Never Over, Never Under

  • Overquote — the client walks. Worse: they may never tell us why.
  • Underquote — we lose margin, and we damage the trust the Ad-Lib confirmation was designed to build.

When uncertainty remains material, the system must express that uncertainty through an explicit range, conditional price, diagnostic path, or other declared pricing mechanism rather than silently assuming favorable conditions.

R-PRICE-REACHESPartialEvery modifier the customer's answer produced must actually reach the price. A formula that consumes one answer does not thereby exempt the other answers from their own effects. The pricing engine excludes only the keys the formula genuinely consumes; every other answer's modifier still applies. The alternative — silently skipping every modifier whenever any formula is active — is a real, confirmed undercharging class and is prohibited.

The Navigation Architecture

The catalog grid is not a flat list of every service. It is a structured descent through category, group, and — for a group whose services split into recognizable shapes — a behavioral-class-driven picker that asks the customer for the fact they already know, not the fact the backend wants. This section states how that descent is built, why it is structured this way, and how it stays inside the component-layer discipline every other part of the system follows. The customer-facing treatment of the picker and its companion tile is in Gateway A and Gateway B; this chapter is the rule's home for the classification and the layer discipline, and the customer chapters reference it rather than restating its content.

Every Group Carries a Routing Mode

Every group in the catalog is classified by the shape of work it contains. The classification is derived from the group's own aggregate module usage; it is not hand-authored per query, and it is the same classification the intake composer and the semantic route validator already consult. There is exactly one classification per group, and every subsystem that needs it reads from that one source.

Routing ModeWhat the customer arrives knowingHow the picker leads
component-firstThe part. (A lock, a lever, a fixture.)"Which one?" — components first; action refined second, only where the group has genuine action multiplicity for the selected component.
symptom-firstThe problem. (Won't spin, leaking, making noise.)"What's happening?" — symptoms first; each symptom resolves through its own real mapping.
parentNothing yet — this group delegates to its children.No picker. Child groups render as they always have.
undeterminedNothing structured — the group has no real signal either way.No picker. The group's structured builder opens directly.

Component and Symptom Are Two Shapes, Not Two Modes

It is tempting to think of component-led and symptom-led as two competing interface modes, and to look for a third for "action." That reading misses what the classification is actually telling us: for a given family of work, one of the two is what the customer arrives with, and the other isn't. A customer thinking about a deadbolt is not going to describe its symptoms; a customer whose refrigerator is making a strange noise is not going to name its compressor. The picker honours the shape the customer is actually holding.

Action is a real, orthogonal axis, but it belongs under the component or symptom choice, not beside them. For most groups, the component already names the action — "deadbolt" is already a noun for "install or replace a deadbolt." Where a group genuinely has more than one real action for a single component, the picker asks the action as a second, real question. Where it doesn't, it doesn't.

P-SYSTEM-PICKER-RENDERPrincipleWhat the Picker Is, and What It Is Not

The picker is a front door onto the canonical resolution. It reads the routing-mode classification and the group's own component and symptom vocabulary, and it renders whatever the customer's own next real choice should be.

The picker is not a resolution pipeline. It does not compose chains, compute confidence, or calculate price. Whatever the customer selects — a specific component, a specific symptom, or the group's "Other" tile — hands off to the same canonical collector and the same orchestrator the direct-tile tap uses. The picker is a Renderer; the resolution is Logic; the two are separate by layer, and the boundary is enforced.

P-SYSTEM-PICKER-MODELPrincipleThe Picker Renders the Customer's Model, Not the Backend's

When several distinct component choices resolve to the same underlying service, the picker still renders each as a genuine, separate option. The customer's mental model is what the picker honours; the fact that the backend converges is an implementation detail the customer never sees and never needs to. Collapsing the picker's own tiles because the backend collapsed the resolution would trade a customer-recognizable choice for an internal convenience — exactly backwards.

This is a durable rule, not a styling preference. A picker that collapses choices to match a backend that has already unified them removes a real, meaningful decision the customer was making. The customer's own reasoning is what the picker exists to capture.

The Picker and Its Companion Tile

Every picker has a companion "Other" tile at the same level. When the picker's own vocabulary does not cover the customer's case — a component the group has not authored, a symptom outside the group's known set, a job that is genuinely a different shape from this family of work — the "Other" tile opens the group-scoped structured builder. The customer-facing treatment of the tile, and why it is a current and permanent part of the product rather than a transitional escape hatch, is in Gateway B — The "Other" Tile. This chapter states the pairing as an engineering fact; the doctrine belongs in Part I, and the two chapters reference each other rather than restating the same argument.

P-SYSTEM-CLASSIFY-ONEPrincipleThe Classification Is a Single Source, Not a Second Copy

The routing-mode classification lives in derived data — a compiler output — and is consumed by every subsystem that needs it: the picker's own shape, the intake composer's branch selection, the semantic check that notices when a resolved service's own chain disagrees with what its group's routing mode would predict. There is exactly one classification, and every consumer reads from that one. A second copy, wherever it appeared, would drift — and that drift is a defect, not a variation.

R-SYSTEM-PICKER-AGREEPartialA picker rule and its routing mode classification are never in disagreement. If the picker would lead with symptoms for a component-first group, either the picker is wrong or the classification is — and the disagreement is a defect to resolve at the source, not a variation to accept downstream.

The Picker Respects the Layer Boundary

The picker is a renderer. It does not own the classification, it does not own the group's vocabulary, and it does not own the resolution. It reads state that the logic layer has already resolved and renders whatever the customer's next real choice is. The classification itself, the component and symptom mappings, and the resolution path all live in the layers their own charter rules assign them to; the picker's only job is to make the customer's next step legible and to hand off the customer's own selection.

When the picker needs to know what to show, it asks the logic layer. When it needs to know what the customer chose, it hands the choice back. It never computes a price, never composes a chain, and never inspects any global UI state to decide what a customer should see.

The NLP Layer

The NLP layer is what turns a client's sentence into a structured request the orchestrator can act on. It is the recognition half of the recognition-and-scoping process that every client walks through; the intake layer is the other half, and the two share more machinery than is obvious from the outside.

The same machinery produces the live "We Understood" preview in Gateway C. As the client types, the composer runs the recognition pass against the current text on every keystroke (or a tight debounce), fills what it can, and renders the result as an editable sentence. That sentence is not a separate summary of what the client typed — it is the system's actual current understanding of the request, expressed in natural English.

R-SYSTEM-ONEPARSEEnforcedOne Parse, One Composer

The input pipeline has exactly one canonical implementation. One function parses the client's words into structured intent. One function composes the sentence back out. Every real entry point — the live preview, the confirm button, the guided builder's close-sync, the builder finish — calls these. There is no second parser, no parallel sentence builder, no exception.

This is not an aesthetic preference. When two parsers exist, they eventually disagree, and the customer is the one who finds out — they watch one understanding compose on screen and get a different one priced at the end. That failure has a name in this project: the preview-vs-charge mismatch. It is the reason the single-parse rule is a structural test, not a policy.

What the Layer Sees

Recognition must be robust to how clients actually write. Real clients type in fragments; they include incidental words; they complain about the app itself mid-request; they use vocabulary the catalog has never seen. The layer must distinguish a stated need from an incidental mention, extract slots from sentences whose word order does not match the template, and route an unfamiliar phrase into the same behavioural machinery as an explicitly named service.

Four concrete defenses exist, each for a real, observed class of error:

  • Quoted and parenthetical content is stripped before scoring. A customer writing "I need a bed assembled (the app keeps suggesting 'drywall or plaster' which is nonsense)" must not be routed on the word plaster. Quoted text is a customer talking about the system, not to it.
  • Matches found beyond the first ~15 words score at half weight. A customer's real intent is overwhelmingly in the head of the sentence; trailing clauses are far more likely to be incidental. The weighting compounds with the synonym half-weight rule below rather than replacing it.
  • A winning candidate whose entire evidence came from the tail region has its named recommendation suppressed. General routing may still proceed; a confident named-service recommendation may not. The system never overstates certainty it does not have.
  • Every promoted synonym is checked against every other entry in the catalog. A synonym earns full confidence only when it is genuinely specific and unambiguous. Refrigerator earns full weight; range, burner, and nest do not, because each is a common English word with real, unrelated meanings.

Two Tag Systems, Not One

The NLP path consults smart_tags. The catalog path consults intent_mappings.objects. These are two distinct systems with overlapping but non-identical tag sets; they are not interchangeable, and they are not the same tag set. Both live in the SSOT as data. The full treatment — what each system answers, why they are distinct, and what any future unification would require — is in The Single Source of Truth.

The Cross-Entry Specificity Rule

Every entry in intent_mappings.objects is scored independently of every other. This is fine until an author writes a natural, customer-friendly multi-word synonym that happens to contain a different entry's bare keyword — e.g. "dish washer" contains "washer", and both are real synonyms for different appliances.

The resolution is not to lower the specificity of either entry. It is to enforce a single, precise rule: when the current winner's own bare keyword appears, as a whole word, inside a different entry's authored multi-word synonym or keyword, and that phrase genuinely appears in the customer's text, the more specific entry wins outright — provided it clears its own confidence bar on its own merits, and provided it is not itself a resolver-bearing entry whose whole job is to re-derive from a shared verb.

This rule is auditable at the data level. The project maintains a permanent detector that recomputes the full catalog-wide risk list on every run and fails loudly the instant a new pair appears. The count of reviewed-and-accepted risk pairs is a real, current number in the record, not a snapshot of a good day.

Grounding in the Catalog's Own Vocabulary

Object extraction is grounded in the SSOT's own vocabulary first, not in a hardcoded word list. When a customer types a noun the catalog already knows, the extractor finds it. When the noun is genuinely new, the extractor falls back to a constrained pattern, and the resulting phrase is evaluated by the same resolver that handles every other real request — no special case, no invented entity.

Two extractions of the same object exist, and they are deliberately distinct:

  • The strict noun — client-visible, held to the catalog's own vocabulary, quoted in the "We Understood" sentence.
  • The lenient noun — internal matching only, wider vocabulary, never quoted to the customer.

Keeping these apart is what allows the system to recognize a wider range of phrasings without promising the customer it understood a word it did not.

The Group-Scoped Inference

Some concepts are inferable from context when the customer's own words would not trigger them directly — a mantel implies brick, a vaulted ceiling implies height. These inferences are real, authored, and group-scoped: a keyword that fires only within a specific group, so that "fireplace" can imply the brick-wall tag in a wall-mounting context without bleeding the same inference into unrelated work. The three-level fact scoping described in the Domain chapter is the same discipline applied to the tag data itself.

An inference that does not carry its group context is dead on arrival. A group-scoped tag whose inference function is called without the group id will silently fail every time, and the failure looks like "the feature was never built" rather than "the feature lost its input." The group id must be threaded through the inference call the same way it is threaded through every other piece of the resolution pipeline.

R-SYSTEM-COMPOSERPartialThe Composer Is a Renderer; the Parser Is Not

All parsing of a client's typed words lives in the NLP layer and nowhere else. The renderer contains zero parsing functions. It renders what the engine returns. This boundary is enforced structurally: the test suite fails if any module-level function is declared twice (the later copy silently shadows the earlier), if the renderer gains a parsing function, or if a module-level initializer reads SSOT state before the SSOT exists.

The structural test is not just a guard against a future mistake. It is the running record of two specific classes of mistake that have already happened: duplicate parsers (the preview pipeline carrying its own object extractor separate from the canonical one) and early-load readers (the builder's room and condition chips computed at script parse time, before the SSOT was available).

R-SYSTEM-SCRIPTPartialScript-tag scope is real. Top-level declarations in one inline <script> block are not visible in another unless explicitly exported. The convention is an established, working export — a module-namespaced global for NLP data and every other shared surface. A function that reads a bare name from a sibling script block will pass every test that concatenates the file's scripts into one scope and fail in a real browser. Any tool that reads the whole file must honour the boundary, not abstract it away.

What the Layer Guarantees

  • The same request reaches the same understanding. One parser, one composer, one set of grounded vocabulary.
  • The live preview is the price. The sentence the customer watches compose is the sentence the price is built from — not a paraphrase, not a re-derivation.
  • An unfamiliar phrase falls through to the same machinery as a familiar one. Dynamic services, group resolution, and the fallback chain are the same paths for a "smart mirror" as for a dishwasher.
  • Weak evidence never masquerades as strong evidence. A match from the tail, a match from a quoted aside, a match from a common-word synonym — each is scored honestly, and none can be promoted into a named-service recommendation on its own.
  • A genuinely unfinished parse is not silently priced. When the parse does not reach a real entity, the composer opens the guided builder rather than inventing a service, and the customer is asked the questions that will complete the understanding.
The layer's whole job, in one sentence: turn whatever the client typed into the same structured intent the rest of the pipeline would have produced if the client had tapped the right service card — and do it without inventing anything the client did not say.

The Single Source of Truth

The SSOT file is the single source of business knowledge. Every rule, fee, intake question, service, and relationship lives there — not in code.

R-SYSTEM-NODATAEnforcedThe Core Rule

No business rule in code that should be in data. If a change to pricing, services, or fees requires touching application code, that is a signal the SSOT is incomplete — not that the code needs the change.
Data Authority. Every real business number lives in the SSOT. When a new number is needed — a fee, rate, threshold, buffer, modifier value, credit policy, or any other coefficient — the agent decides it using the hierarchy in Decision Authority and Agent Autonomy, anchors it to existing catalog data or NYC market rate where possible, and documents the reasoning in the field's own _note. Flagging for operator review is the last resort in that hierarchy, not the first response. The original intent of this rule — that ungrounded numbers must not silently enter the catalog — remains fully in force; the change is that "ungrounded" is defined by the hierarchy, not by the absence of operator sign-off.

R-SYSTEM-DECLARATIVEEnforcedDeclarative Only

The SSOT declares knowledge. It does not contain JavaScript and it does not become a hidden application runtime.

R-SYSTEM-SHAPEEnforcedContent Is Protected; Shape Is a Design Decision

The SSOT has two things the Charter has previously conflated: content and shape. They are governed differently.

The SSOT's content — the services, fees, chains, rules, tags, materials, formulas — is hand-curated, authoritative, and protected by every rule in this chapter. No compiler overwrites it. No session silently edits it. It is the source of business truth.

The SSOT's shape — the namespace structure, the key layout, whether a fact lives on a service or on the group that owns it, whether derived data sits on the node it describes or in a separate compiled namespace — is a design decision. It is not content. It is not hand-curated business knowledge. It is the container the content sits in.

When the shape forces a consumer to work around it — by copying a fact into a second location, by maintaining a parallel representation, by skipping a lookup it would naturally perform, by merging at the call site what could have been merged at the source — the shape is the defect. The correct response is to change the shape, not to add the workaround. This is R-GOVERN-TRANSITION applied to the SSOT specifically.

The failure this rule exists to prevent: a project in which the SSOT's shape is treated as a fixed given, and every other part of the architecture is built around it. The architecture's quality becomes bounded by the shape's quality, and the shape is never improved because improving it looks like a violation. That is a stable, self-reinforcing failure mode, and it is what this rule names and forbids.

Architecture must evolve with the authoritative model. Regression protection must prevent recurrence of incorrect behavior, not prevent legitimate structural change. When the domain model or SSOT shape is corrected, dependent architecture, code, tests, and derived representations must be allowed — and required where applicable — to evolve with it.

Derived Data Has a Different Lifecycle

Compiler output is a derived artifact, not a second SSOT. It may provide indexes, routing structures, or other executable forms of the declarative knowledge, but it does not become an independent source of business truth. Do not hand-edit it. Do not regenerate it without confirming the compiler can reproduce everything it currently contains. If it cannot, the compiler is wrong or incomplete; the missing knowledge does not become disposable merely because it appeared in derived data.

P-SYSTEM-NOEDITPrincipleThe compiler may derive structure; it may not overwrite curated content. When existing, hand-curated content is present for a group, the compiler preserves it outright — not merge, not average, not re-derive. Fresh derivation is used only as a genuine fallback for entities that have nothing to protect. This is the rule that prevents a routine compiler re-run from silently reclassifying a group the catalog deliberately classified. Clarification (placement vs. lifecycle). The rule governs lifecycle — derived data is not hand-edited, is not a second SSOT, and is fully regenerable — not location. Derived data may live either on the node it describes (as a compiler-owned, underscore-prefixed key) or in a dedicated compiled namespace; both placements are compliant, and the choice is a design decision, not a governance one. What the rule prohibits is treating a derived artifact as authoritative — regardless of where it sits.

P-SYSTEM-VERSIONSPrincipleTwo Version Counters, One Discipline

The SSOT tracks two version numbers separately, and the distinction is load-bearing:

  • Content version — bumps on any meaningful content edit: new services, retagged smart tags, new modules, adjusted thresholds.
  • Schema version — bumps only when the shape of the file changes in a way that would make old code misread it: renamed keys, removed keys, new required fields, changed nesting.

An additive change — a new optional field, a new namespace — does not bump the schema version, because old code that ignores an unrecognized field still reads the file correctly. A structural change does. The two counters answer different questions, and conflating them hides the difference between "the content moved" and "the file's contract changed."

Validation

Every change to the SSOT must validate against its schema. The schema is not self-enforcing — a comment claiming validation was run is not the same as running the validator. A validator that prints an error and exits zero is not validating; it is narrating.

The specific failure mode: a schema validator whose exit code never reflects its own findings. Every call must fail loudly and with a non-zero exit code when the SSOT is invalid, so that an automated check that only looks at the exit code actually catches the problem. Reference-check advisories — orphan modules, unused fields — may remain non-blocking; genuine schema violations may not.

P-SYSTEM-TAGSPrincipleTwo Tag Systems, Not One

The NLP input path uses smart_tags from the SSOT. The catalog search path uses intent_mappings.objects. These are two distinct systems with overlapping but non-identical tag/keyword sets. Both live in the SSOT as data. They are not the same tag set, and the charter does not claim they are. Any future unification must be done as one deliberate change, with a migration path and a ledger entry.

Compiler boundary. The compiler may derive structure; it may not silently invent business knowledge. Derived artifacts have a different lifecycle from their source: they are not a second SSOT and are not freely regenerable. If compiler output contains hand-curated knowledge that the compiler cannot reproduce, the compiler is incomplete and the source knowledge must not be discarded.

Four Orthogonal Dimensions

The orchestrator composes four independent dimensions. This is the single most important architectural decision in the project: it is what allows the system to scale without coupling.

ConcernDefinesDoes not define
Routing ModeHow confidence is builtThe UI or pricing
Pricing ModeHow cost is calculatedThe service itself

Checkout StateHow the price is presented to the clientWhat the price is

A service's routing can change without touching its price; its price can change without touching its route; its presentation can change without touching either. A new entity can inherit useful behavior across all four dimensions without any subsystem knowing the others exist.

Two Clarifications

Entity is the conceptual primitive: the thing in the world being discussed (for example, a Door Lever, Window, or Toilet). It is the same conceptual entity used by the Domain Model; the Four Dimensions do not introduce a second "Item Primitive" concept.

Checkout State is a presentation/output concept, not a pricing method. Pricing determines the monetary result; checkout state determines how that result is expressed to the client (for example, exact, ranged, conditional, or diagnostic). It therefore belongs to the output side of the pricing pipeline while remaining separate from the pricing calculation itself.

Separation of Concerns

  • Knowledge is declarative only. No JavaScript inside the SSOT file.
  • Extracted standalone modules must stay in sync with their source. Any change to the source must be followed by re-extraction and a parity check.
  • No duplicated implementations. If two functions solve the same problem, one must be canonical and the others delegate.
  • Legacy code must be explicitly marked. Bug fixes are permitted; new features are not.
  • Any list, constant, or registry used by more than one function must be defined once. A duplicated definition is a confirmed recurring bug class.

Rendering Rules

  • All renderers are pure functions of resolved state.
  • State mutations go through the canonical dispatch path.
  • R-SYSTEM-NODOMEnforcedNo direct innerHTML with untrusted input. Use safe DOM creation methods.
  • Event handlers must not contain business logic.
  • Renderers must clear containers before re-rendering — stale content is a real, confirmed bug class.

Security Criteria

  • No dynamic evaluation of user input — no eval, no new Function, no string-argument setTimeout.
  • The SSOT is trusted; user input is not.
  • R-SYSTEM-NOREFEnforcedNo $ref or modifier-reference strings are ever executed. They are data.
  • R-SYSTEM-CSPEnforcedCSP-compatible. Inline script blocks are avoided where possible.

Engineering Invariants

These are the rules that should survive implementation changes.

R-INVARIANT-SOURCEEnforcedOne Source of TruthSee Battle →

Business knowledge belongs in the SSOT.

R-INVARIANT-CANONICALPartialOne Canonical Implementation per BehaviorSee Battle →

If two functions answer the same real question differently, one is eventually going to drift. A canonical resolver is only canonical if it is the last step. A caller-side OR, whitelist, or re-derivation on top of a canonical resolver is a second implementation, regardless of whether the resolver itself was duplicated. Extract shared logic; make other callers delegate.

R-INVARIANT-PROVENANCEPartialResolution Provenance Travels on the Return ValueSee Battle →

A resolution's source travels on the return value. A function that resolves a concept returns a record carrying both the value and which branch supplied it (source: 'service_override' | 'archetype_default' | 'dynamic_engine' | 'fallback' | 'answer_override'; answer_override names the case where the customer's own answer superseded the resolved value, and is set where that answer is applied, so a resolver whose branches do not include one never returns it). Callers read the resolved value from the resolver's return value. A caller that composes a resolver's return with a second source — a || chain, an ?? chain, a whitelist that selects between pre-computed candidates, or an inline re-derivation of the same concept — has reintroduced arbitration outside the resolver, and that is the defect this rule prohibits. A "plausible number" that cannot be traced to a named source on its own return value is a defect, not a success.

Why one rule does the work of three. It forces resolvers to declare their choice, and it forbids the caller-side composition layer that has been the actual source of divergence. Because provenance is carried in the return value rather than reconstructed by callers, there is exactly one place a resolution's origin can be read from — the resolver's own output — and no caller has the information needed to re-derive it. See Named Defect Classes — Arbitration.

R-INVARIANT-DUPLICATION-TICKETPartialEvery Second Implementation Is TicketedSee Battle →

Whenever a second implementation of an existing behavior is added — even intentionally, even as a transitional measure — the first implementation is filed with a decommissioning ticket in the same commit. No transitional duplicate persists un-ticketed. A transitional duplicate that is touched by a subsequent bug fix must either be promoted (with the promotion and the deletion of the original acknowledged in the same change) or the fix must be applied to the replacement instead.

This is the mechanism that produced the current state. Each session correctly chose to fix the bug in the code that was running; each such fix made the legacy path more load-bearing; nothing forced a decision at the moment of the fix. This rule forces that decision.

P-INVARIANT-RENDERERSPrincipleNo Business Logic in RenderersSee Battle →

Renderers consume resolved state and create UI. They do not independently resolve services, calculate pricing, or recreate confidence formulas.

P-INVARIANT-HANDLERSPrincipleNo Business Logic in Event HandlersSee Battle →

Events dispatch actions. Controllers delegate. Engines decide.

R-INVARIANT-NOEVALEnforcedNo Unsafe DOM Injection

  • User-provided text is untrusted.
  • Use safe DOM APIs and escaping.
  • No dynamic evaluation of user input.
  • Reference strings are data, not executable code.

P-INVARIANT-NOLEGACYPrincipleNo Silent Legacy ExpansionSee Battle →

A legacy function may need a bug fix while it remains load-bearing. It must not become the place where new business behavior accumulates.

R-INVARIANT-DELETIONEnforcedA Retirement Is Deleted, Not Deprecated

A retirement is complete when the retired artifact is deleted from source control. Deprecated-but-present is not a state. Deprecated-but-still-called is a defect. Any declaration of completion for an architectural migration must cite the specific removal, or it does not use the word "complete."

Compliance before deletion. When existing code is non-compliant but still load-bearing, the first response is to attempt to adopt or adapt that code to the Charter's compliant architecture. If no compliant substitute or comparable implementation yet exists, the load-bearing code should be rewritten toward compliance rather than reflexively deleted. Deletion becomes the next step when the code has been successfully superseded, when a compliant replacement exists, or when a deliberate assessment establishes that retaining it cannot be justified. The point is to retire non-compliant architecture deliberately, not to make the metric look clean by deleting the mechanism before its consequences are understood.

This rule exists because prior sessions declared an architectural migration complete while the code remained present. It also prevents the opposite failure: treating deletion itself as progress when the deleted code still carries necessary behavior. A completion claim must account for both sides — compliance or a verified replacement, and actual removal of the retired artifact.

P-INVARIANT-NOPATCHPrincipleNo Path-Specific PatchingSee Battle →

If a behavior is correct, it belongs in the canonical resolution architecture. A fix that exists only on one entry path is a temporary symptom fix, not the finished behavior.

Governance implication. No fix may be applied as an ad-hoc patch to one of the parallel gateways. Any correct behavior discovered in a legacy path must be ported into the orchestrator, verified as equivalent across all three gateways, and only then may the legacy path be retired.

P-INVARIANT-VERIFYPrincipleVerify the Complete Relevant SetSee Battle →

A check is only as complete as the mechanisms and population it considers. A one-case regression test proves that one case works. It does not prove the catalog, all extraction paths, all renderers, or all dynamic lookup mechanisms remain correct.

R-INVARIANT-ONEUNDERSTANDINGEnforcedOne Understanding, EverywhereSee Battle →

There is one parse of the client's request and one composer of the sentence. Every render path — live preview, confirm, curated card, self-quote, builder — reads from the same canonical intent. A second parser is a defect, regardless of how similar its output looks today.

R-INVARIANT-ANCHOREnforcedAnchor Discipline

Cross-document references must cite anchors, not section numbers or other unstable identifiers. A reference to a Playbook anchor that does not exist is a broken reference and is treated as a defect, not a stylistic variance.

P-INVARIANT-CHIPSNOTFEESPrincipleChips Are Not Pricing FactorsSee Battle →

No chip carries a fee, appears in a price breakdown, or affects the quote in its own right. Any code path that reads a chip as a fee source is a defect. Fees live on answers; answers live on the booking context; the pricing engine reads answers, not chips.

Principle 9 — Converge, verify, preserve.Converge means all three gateways produce the same canonical BookingContext and pricing result for equivalent facts. Verify means test the complete relevant mechanisms and population rather than trusting a local success. Preserve means retain source knowledge, historical evidence, and hand-curated SSOT content when deriving or refactoring implementation structures.

P-INVARIANT-PROPAGATEPrincipleChange Propagation

Change propagation is part of correctness. When an authoritative source changes, every derived representation that depends on it must be regenerated or updated before the change is considered complete. A stale projection of current authority is a data-integrity defect, not an acceptable intermediate state.

P-INVARIANT-DONEPrincipleDefinition of Done

Done means all four: the mechanism is corrected; the complete relevant set has been verified to the extent relevant to the change; every dependent derived representation is synchronized; and appropriate regression protection exists. Regression protection may be a class detector, an existing authoritative contract test, or documented one-off evidence when no permanent test is justified.

Regression Prevention Discipline

Regression protection protects intent, not history. A regression artifact exists to prevent recurrence of incorrect behavior or to protect a current authoritative contract. It does not grant permanent authority to the implementation, architecture, data shape, or historical behavior that originally produced it. When the authoritative model or intended behavior legitimately changes, affected tests are re-evaluated and may be rewritten, migrated, subsumed, or retired.

The Charter previously required a permanent regression test for every fix. That produced hundreds of instance tests and made the codebase fragile. The replacement is to protect recurring defect classes and authoritative intent without freezing historical implementation details.

R-INVARIANT-DISEASEEnforcedEvery fix receives regression protection appropriate to its defect class. A recurring defect extends an existing class detector or creates a new one; a genuine one-off is documented without a permanent test. A detector protects the defect class, not the implementation that happened to fix one instance.

There are two kinds of regression artifacts, and they have different lifecycles:

KindWhat it guardsHow many existWhen it changes
Class detectorA general shape — "no per-visit fee is ever multiplied by quantity," "no second parser exists," "no resolver return is composed with a second source at the call site"One per named defect class; the live count belongs in the regression ledgerWhen the class's signature sharpens, or when the underlying mechanism changes enough that the detector's own premise changes
One-off evidenceA specific instance used to diagnose or document a defectTemporary or ledger-onlyRetained only when it has independent value; it is not automatically promoted to a permanent regression test

The class detector is the disease cure. The one-off assertion is the symptom treatment. The prior rule mandated the second for every instance of the first, without distinguishing the two.

How a fix is dispositioned

  1. Recognize the class. When a bug is diagnosed, the first question is which named defect class it is an instance of. The named classes are in Part X.
  2. If it is an instance of a named class, the detector for that class is extended — never a new one-off test. The class's own detector file grows.
  3. If it is an instance of no named class, and the shape is likely to recur, a new class is named and its detector is created. This is the mechanism by which the class list grows.
  4. If it is a genuine one-off — a data error, a typo, a specific config mistake — it is documented in the owning ledger entry and produces no permanent test. Its absence from the test suite is correct, not a gap.
The number of tests grows with the number of real classes, not with the number of bugs. A codebase with eight well-chosen class detectors is more robust than one with eight hundred instance assertions, and it is orders of magnitude less fragile.

Test retirement

G-INVARIANT-RETIRE-TESTGovernance ProcessRetire a test when it is subsumed by a broader class detector, guards retired behavior, or is over-fit to an instance the current contract no longer requires.

Test retirement is an affirmative act, not a violation. The discipline parallels R-INVARIANT-DELETION: a retirement is deleted, not deprecated. The suite's growth is bounded by the number of real classes, not by the number of changes the project has ever made.

What remains true

G-INVARIANT-PREFIXGovernance ProcessWhen a new class detector is created, run it against the pre-fix code first. A class detector that passes both before and after the fix it was written for is not yet detecting the class — it may be detecting a symptom of the class. This is a discipline, not a mechanically enforceable rule.

R-INVARIANT-REDTESTEnforcedA red test is either fixed within one working session or formally retired with a written reason in the test's own header. The meta-test asserts no test file is marked as a persistent failure across more than one session's ledger entries. "It's a pre-existing failure" and "it doesn't block anything customer-facing" are not valid dispositions.

G-INVARIANT-SWEEPGovernance ProcessWhen a class is discovered, sweep every sibling mechanism the class could reach before declaring the defect contained. The class detector is a consequence of the sweep; the sweep is the act. Detectors are what make future sweeps unnecessary.

R-INVARIANT-SINGLEDEFEnforcedAny list, constant, or registry used by more than one function is defined once. A shared constant used by more than one function is a single, module-level definition, with a test asserting no second copy exists.

R-INVARIANT-RERUNEnforcedAny change to the SSOT triggers a compiler re-run, and the derived data is merged in. The compiler itself is re-runnable; drift between raw and compiled is a defect, not a tolerated state.

The Pre-Change Checklist

Before modifying a significant part of the system, in order:

  1. Run the full test suite. Know the current pass state before touching anything.
  2. Search the ledger. Check TIMELINE.md and PENDING_DECISIONS.md — has it been fixed before? Deliberately deferred? Superseded?
  3. Check the component map. Before touching anything that looks like legacy code, read COMPONENT_LAYER_MAP.md.
  4. Run a real validator if a comment claims one was run. The schema is not self-enforcing.
  5. Sweep the full catalog for any change affecting routing or pricing.
  6. After the change: re-run the suite, re-sweep the catalog, and update the ledger.

Goodhart's Law and the Measurement Rule

The Charter's rules are meant to protect outcomes and architecture, not to create a game in which passing the test becomes the objective. When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. A test can therefore become actively harmful when developers optimize the source to satisfy the test's observable shape instead of satisfying the rule the test was intended to protect.

P-GOVERN-GOODHARTPrincipleMeasure the Rule by Its Own Mechanism

Test behavior where the rule is behavioral. Test source where the rule is about source. Never invent a source-shape test for a behavioral rule.

Every Charter rule should be validated according to the kind of claim it makes. The validation mechanism must follow from the rule itself, not from whatever check is easiest to automate. A green test suite is evidence of compliance with the tested claims; it is not authority to preserve an obsolete architecture or behavior.

Rule categoryWhat the rule claimsCorrect validation mechanism
Behavioral“X does Y.”Drive the real interface and observe the real output. Do not require a particular internal implementation.
Interface contract“X exists and has shape Y.”Assert the interface exists and behaves at its boundary. Do not prescribe the internal algorithm.
Source-shape“No X exists in source” or equivalent source structure is itself the rule.Inspect the source. The source check is the rule's own mechanism.
Retired artifact“The retired artifact is absent.”Assert absence. Presence is not evidence of successful retirement.

A compliant test validates the rule using the rule's own mechanism. A distorting test validates the rule using an invented mechanism that is merely convenient to check. The difference matters because a green test can otherwise certify the wrong architecture.

This is especially important for architectural migrations. A test that requires a particular helper, function name, regex, data structure, or source pattern can turn an implementation detail into a hidden specification. If the Charter's actual rule is behavioral, a compliant refactor should remain free to change that implementation while preserving the observed behavior.

Source inspection is not inherently wrong. It is correct when the rule itself is about source shape — for example, absence of unsafe evaluation, absence of retired artifacts, or a deliberate layer boundary. The error is using source shape as a proxy for a behavioral outcome merely because source shape is easier to test.

Three named lessons reinforce this without becoming new enforcement tiers:Chesterton's Fence — understand load-bearing behavior before removing it; Lehman's Laws of Software Evolution — useful software must continue to evolve rather than become frozen by preservation; Hyrum's Law — observable behavior can acquire dependencies whether or not those dependencies were intended. These names are explanatory labels for lessons already embodied here, not additional Charter rules.

Goodhart's practical question: “If someone optimized solely to make this test pass, could they satisfy the test while violating the Charter rule?” If yes, the test is measuring the wrong thing or is missing the behavioral assertion that makes the measurement meaningful.

Named Defect Classes

Individual bugs are patched. Recurring bug classes are named — and every name is a standing instruction to build a detector. Naming a class is what turns G-INVARIANT-DETECTOR from an aspiration into an action: "this bug is an instance of <class>; the detector for <class> must now exist." That is the mechanism that stops whack-a-mole. A class whose detector does not yet exist is itself an open item under G-INVARIANT-DETECTOR.

P-GOVERN-DEFECTCLASSPrincipleWhen a bug recurs, it acquires a name. A recurring defect class is listed in this section with its distinguishing signature, the invariants it violates, and the detector it now demands. The list is append-only in the sense that a class is never removed — it may be marked "detector shipped" or "detector pending," but the name persists so that future instances can be recognized instantly rather than re-diagnosed from scratch.

Why a Named Class and Not Just a Battle

The Battle narratives record what happened and why, chronologically. A named class is the durable pattern those Battles are instances of. The two are complementary, not redundant: the Battle is the evidence, the class is the search key. When a new bug arrives, the first question is no longer "have I seen this before?" — it is "which named class is this an instance of?" Once the class is identified, the owning invariant is known, and the detector either fires (if it already exists) or is demanded (if it does not).

How a Class Becomes a Detector

The path from a new named class to an enforced detector is the same every time:

  1. Name the class. The first time a bug is recognized as a recurrence of an existing pattern, or the first time a genuinely new pattern is recognized as likely to recur, a row is added to the table above. The name is stable; the description may sharpen.
  2. Identify the owning invariants. Which of the Charter's existing rules does this class violate? A class with no owning invariant is either a rule the Charter is missing (and the Charter must be amended) or a bug that is not actually a class (and should be filed as a one-off instance).
  3. Specify the detector. What mechanism, run on every test, distinguishes an instance of this class from a passing system? The detector's shape follows from the class's signature. If the detector can be a source-shape test and the class is genuinely about source shape, so be it; if the class is behavioral, the detector must drive the real interface (see P-GOVERN-GOODHART).
  4. Ship the detector. A class whose detector does not yet exist is an open item. A class whose detector exists but is not in the CI gate is also an open item. The row's "Detector" cell states the current state honestly.
The load-bearing claim.The reason whack-a-mole happens is that bug instances get patched while bug classes go unnamed. Naming a class converts the next instance from a diagnosis into a lookup. The arbitration class — the one this revision adds — is the clearest case in the project's history: the defect persisted across years of governance precisely because no name existed for the shape of the bug, so every instance was argued about on its own terms rather than recognized as the same failure mode returning.

The Arbitration Class, Specifically

Arbitration is the class this section was primarily added to name, and its relationship to the rest of the Charter is worth stating once and clearly:

  • The defect: caller-side composition of a resolver's return with a second source. The resolver correctly chooses; the caller then re-chooses.
  • Why it survived so long: each individual call site looked like a reasonable safety net — a fallback for when the resolver returned nothing, a whitelist to constrain the resolver's output, an inline re-derivation to "verify" it. No single site looked like a bug. The shape only becomes visible when named.
  • Why R-INVARIANT-PROVENANCE fixes it: once the resolver's return value carries its own source, callers no longer have the information needed to re-choose — the resolved value already declares where it came from, and composing it with a second source is structurally a second resolution. The rule moves the decision to exactly one place.
  • Why the detector must exist: the shape is easy to add back. A future caller looking for a fallback will reach for resolver() || fallback unless something fails loudly. The provenance-field check is that something.

Glossary

TermDefinition
BookingContextThe canonical convergence contract containing the resolved facts needed by downstream pricing, presentation, and booking. All three gateways converge into this context.
ResolvedRouteThe orchestrator's resolved execution result. It is related to, but not an alias for, BookingContext; the exact object shape belongs to the owning implementation specification.
EntityThe conceptual thing in the world being discussed, such as a Door Lever, Window, or Toilet. Product-specific details are attributes.
Behavioral ClassThe domain-model class formerly called an archetype: a reusable behavioral classification that tells the system how an entity is generally understood and handled.
Routing Mode / historical implementation namingThe orthogonal concern describing how confidence and scope are built. The compiled SSOT carries this as routing_archetypes; the key name does not redefine the conceptual term.
Component / Symptom / ActionThe three real axes a picker can descend through. Component and Symptom are shapes — the group's routing mode determines which one leads, because that is what the customer arrives with for this family of work. Action is a secondary refinement under the component or symptom, asked only where the group genuinely has more than one real action.
Pricing Mode / historical implementation namingThe orthogonal concern describing how cost is calculated. The live implementation field names remain owned by the SSOT/schema; the aggregate pricing_archetypes namespace is documentation, not runtime.
CapabilityA reusable statement of what can meaningfully happen to or with an entity.
ActionThe concrete operation instantiated for the current job.
StrategyThe method by which the system gathers or infers evidence needed to resolve work.
Derived ServiceA customer-facing expression of resolved scope; it is derived from the domain model rather than serving as its foundation.
Dynamic ServiceA priceable resolution composed at runtime from the catalog's parts, not authored as its own named service entry. Keyed by category+service_type or category+group+service_type.
merge_strategyA dynamic service's declared relationship to its owning group's defaults. "extend" means the dynamic service's own chain adds to the group's; a future "replace" would override it.
SSOTThe single source of business knowledge — every rule, fee, intake question, service, and relationship lives there, declared as data, not code.
CᵢIntent confidence, normalized to 0–1. It expresses how strongly the system understands what the client wants.
CₓScope confidence, normalized to 0–1. It expresses how strongly the system knows the physical/work conditions required for the active pricing path.
τₓThe required scope-confidence threshold for the active resolution/pricing policy. It is an explicit policy input, not a universal scalar-complexity formula.
Uncertainty classA condition whose answer could materially affect price, labor, routing, scheduling, or preparation. Confidence is measured as the proportion of relevant uncertainty classes retired — not as a probability of correctness, and not as a count of questions answered.
FrictionThe remaining scope-confidence gap, represented by max(0, τₓ − Cₓ). It does not replace intent routing or safety checks. Friction is the cost the customer pays to retire uncertainty; the platform's economic goal is the minimum friction necessary to retire the uncertainty the intended pricing precision requires.
PrecisionThe specificity of the priced outcome presented to the client: exact quote, bounded range, conditional quote, or registered diagnostic path.
Checkout StateThe presentation state that communicates how the resolved price is justified. It does not calculate the price.
Smart tagsSSOT data used by the NLP/input path to represent recognized concepts and facts. They are not pricing factors themselves.
Fact scopingThe three narrowing levels — category, group, service exclusion — at which a smart tag's applicability is declared. The exclusion layer exists because a real group can legitimately contain both the fact-applying and the fact-not-applying case.
intent_mappings.objectsThe distinct catalog-search mapping system. It overlaps with smart tags but is not the same tag set.
full_weight_synonymsA per-entry list of synonyms that are as specific and unambiguous as the entry's own keyword. A listed synonym scores at the entry's full confidence_weight; an unlisted synonym scores at half weight. Promotion is earned by specificity, not by usefulness.
High-density evidenceEvidence that can reliably establish multiple independent facts relevant to routing, scope, pricing, scheduling, or preparation under the active service rules — a photograph, a model number, a manufacturer label. The platform prefers high-density evidence over equivalent low-density questionnaires when service rules permit.
Resolution provenanceThe named source of a resolved value, carried on the resolver's own return value (source: 'service_override' | 'archetype_default' | 'dynamic_engine' | 'fallback' | 'answer_override'; the last names a customer answer that superseded the resolved value). A resolved value that cannot be traced to such a source on its own return value is a defect under R-INVARIANT-PROVENANCE.
Named defect classA recurring bug pattern — as distinct from a single bug instance — catalogued in Named Defect Classes with its signature, owning invariants, and detector. The first entry is DEFECT-ARBITRATION. Naming a class is what makes G-INVARIANT-DETECTOR actionable.
Rule codeA stable, flat identifier of the form R-<DOMAIN>-<NAME> attached to every citable rule in the Charter. Codes survive amendments; rule text does not. Used by conversation, by tests (a rule marked Enforced or Partial must have a test file whose name contains the lowercased code), and by the amendment workflow when an operator override is recorded.
Amendment logThe collapsed <details> block under an amended rule. Preserves the original text struck through, the new text, the date, and an "Operator Override — Authorized" tag. The top of the page always shows the current rule; the history is one click below.
T-numberA historical entry reference in TIMELINE.md, used by battle evidence trails.

Decision Hierarchy — Quick Reference

When a business number, fee, threshold, or policy value is needed:

StepSourceWhen to useDocumentation
1NYC market averageA comparable real-world rate existsCite the source inline in the _note
2Existing catalog dataA comparable service, modifier, rate, or fee existsAnchor value + reference in the _note
3Midpoint of high/low rangeEvidence supports a range but no direct anchorRecord the range in the _note
4Best-considered estimateNone of the above produces an answerDocument the reasoning in the _note
Defer only if: the decision would contradict an explicit or clearly-implicit operator instruction. Surface the conflict; do not silently resolve it.
Do not defer because the agent lacks perfect information. That is the normal condition under which the hierarchy exists.

Document Scope

The Playbook is the durable North Star: vision, principles, architectural boundaries, business invariants, and the lessons that should survive implementation changes.

It is not the field-level schema, not a tutorial, and not a task roadmap. Current code state, contracts, ownership, open decisions, and historical evidence live in the engineering documents named in the Document Relationship Map above. When those sources disagree with this Playbook, they take precedence — and the disagreement itself is a tracked engineering issue, not something resolved by silently editing one document to make the conflict disappear.
